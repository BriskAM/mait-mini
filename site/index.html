    How it works

    From raw data to a model you can interrogate

    1. Verify the data

      The 320 MB archive is checked against its published checksum, and the counts are asserted: 39,209 training images, 12,630 test images, 43 classes.

    2. Study it

      Classes are imbalanced (10.7× between largest and smallest). Signs are small (median 43 px wide) and lighting varies from almost black to washed out.

    3. Split by physical sign

      The training images are 1,307 physical signs photographed about 30 times each. A random split puts 1,291 of those signs on both sides, which inflates validation. The split used here holds out whole signs: zero are shared, and every class is represented.

    4. Train two models

      A small CNN built from scratch, and MobileNetV2 with ImageNet weights trained in two stages (new head, then fine-tuning). Normalisation and augmentation live inside the model.

    5. Evaluate once

      The final model is chosen on validation accuracy. The official test set is scored once, with accuracy, macro-F1, top-3 accuracy and calibration.

    6. Explain and stress-test

      Grad-CAM shows where the model looks, a calibration analysis shows when to trust it, and corruption tests show what breaks it.

    The weights were exported from the trained Keras model and the network is re-implemented here in plain JavaScript, with a hand-written Grad-CAM backward pass. The "Verify against Keras" button checks it against the notebook's predictions; offline, it matched Keras to about 3×10-6 in probability on all 116 images. MobileNetV2 and the ensemble appear in the charts but are not loaded here: they are larger and scored lower.

    Grad-CAM here backpropagates the class score before the softmax, as in the original paper. The notebook uses the post-softmax probability, whose gradient vanishes when the model is nearly certain (for roughly 40% of the samples here), which makes those maps numerically unreliable.

    Key design decisions

    Grouped validation split
    Frames of one sign are near-duplicates. Splitting by sign avoids leakage and gives an honest estimate for unseen signs.
    Macro-F1 beside accuracy
    Class sizes differ tenfold; macro-F1 stops common classes hiding weak rare ones.
    64×64 cached images
    The median sign is only a few dozen pixels wide, so more resolution adds cost without information.
    No horizontal flips
    Flipping turns "turn left ahead" into something that looks like "turn right ahead" but keeps the old label.
    Preprocessing inside the model
    The exported model takes raw pixels, so there is no separate preprocessing code to drift out of sync.
    Global average pooling
    Far fewer parameters than flatten-and-dense: the final CNN has 116 K parameters, the basic one 1.63 M, and is 4 points worse.
    Fine-tune, with BatchNorm frozen
    A frozen ImageNet backbone scored about 75% on the test set; fine-tuning lifted it to about 91%.
    Select on validation, test once
    No decision used the test set, so the headline number is an unbiased estimate.
    Abstain when unsure
    A confidence threshold trades coverage for accuracy, a practical safety mechanism.

    Limits and honesty

    What this is not

    Limitations

    • Cropped signs only. It classifies a tight crop of one sign. Finding signs in a street scene needs a separate detector.
    • One country, one dataset. GTSRB is German; other regions' signs are untested.
    • Single training run. Two runs of this pipeline differed by about half a point, so small differences between models are noise.
    • Weak to noise and occlusion. The model was not trained on them; the robustness chart shows the cost.
    • Not safety-certified. This is an educational research project. Do not use it for driving or any safety-critical decision.

    Reproduce and extend

    • Open the Colab notebook and run all cells on a T4 GPU (about 25 minutes of training in full mode).
    • Rebuild this site's data from a results bundle: python scripts/build_pages_data.py --results <folder>.
    • Check the in-browser network against Keras: node scripts/verify_pages_model.mjs.
    • Ideas next: noise and occlusion augmentation, brightness normalisation, a higher input resolution, several seeds.

    Data: J. Stallkamp, M. Schlipsing, J. Salmen and C. Igel, "The German Traffic Sign Recognition Benchmark: A multi-class classification competition", IJCNN 2011. Test images shown here are cropped samples from that benchmark, used for illustration.
